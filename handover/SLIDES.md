## Slide 3 — Your Section 4 claim is correct, and now proven

**On the slide**

> **Claim in the note:** minimising UV auto-correlation minimises PSF sidelobes
>
> **Status: proven**
>
> - Follows from Parseval's theorem
> - Dirty-beam energy = sum of squared gridded sampling counts
> - Verified numerically: ratio = **1.000000000000**
>
> Why it matters: this is what licenses the whole objective

**Speaker notes**

The note states that if we minimise how much the UV sampling overlaps with
itself, we minimise the sidelobes in the point spread function. That is true,
and it is not obvious — those sound like two different goals.

The proof goes through Parseval's theorem. The energy in the dirty beam, which
is what sidelobes are, equals the sum over UV cells of the squared number of
samples in each cell. So "minimise sidelobe energy" and "minimise UV
overlap" are literally the same number.

I checked it numerically on a real layout. The ratio of the two quantities is
one, to twelve decimal places. Without this result, the objective we are
optimising would be a proxy. With it, it is the thing itself.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/src/thz_opt/qubo/baseline_qubo.py — function `sidelobe_energy_terms`

## Slide 4 — Your Q_ij recipe cannot be written as stated

**On the slide**

> **The problem:** baseline-overlap terms need **four** antenna indices
> (i, j, k, l). `Q_ij` has **two** slots.
>
> Written directly, the objective is **quartic** in the pad variables —
> not a QUBO.
>
> **The fix:**
> - One activation variable `y_k` per antenna pair
> - Enforce `y_k = x_i · x_j` via Rosenberg quadratization
> - Overlap terms become plain `y_k y_l` couplings → quadratic again
>
> **Cost:** M + M(M−1)/2 variables. For all 174 ALMA pads, ~15,000.

**Speaker notes**

This is the core technical contribution.

Whether two baselines overlap in the UV plane depends on which two antennas
form the first baseline and which two form the second — four antennas in
total. A term in Q_ij can only reference two. So the recipe in the note cannot
hold these terms. Written out honestly, the objective is fourth-order in the
pad variables, and a QUBO must be second-order.

The standard fix is to introduce a new variable for each antenna pair that
says whether that baseline is active. Then overlap between two baselines is a
product of two of these new variables — quadratic. The catch is forcing the
new variable to actually equal the product of the two pad variables, and that
is done with a penalty term due to Rosenberg.

The penalty weights are derived, not guessed. The code computes the smallest
weight that provably cannot be cheated, and there is a test showing that below
that weight the constraint breaks.

The cost is more variables. For the full ALMA field that is about fifteen
thousand, which matters for the hardware discussion later.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/src/thz_opt/qubo/baseline_qubo.py — functions `build_baseline_qubo`, `rosenberg_penalty_floor`, `selection_penalty_floor`

## Slide 6 — What I built around the formulation

**On the slide**

> A formulation alone cannot be evaluated. So:
>
> - **Optimisers** — greedy, local search, simulated annealing
> - **Exact solver** — mixed-integer programming with a proven dual bound
> - **QUBO solver** — `dwave-samplers`, the same interface that drives D-Wave hardware
> - **Science objective** — Fisher information on a real source, replacing an invented metric
> - **Imaging** — sky → visibilities → noise → CLEAN → compare to truth

**Speaker notes**

When I started, the code could score a layout but had no way to search for a
good one. So the first thing was to build optimisers, and crucially an exact

step. Everything has tests. Two hundred and seventy-two of them pass.

## Slide 7 — Finding 1: the QUBO is correct

**On the slide**

> **Is the formulation right?**
>
> On a 16-pad instance of real ALMA geometry, I enumerated **all 2,324
> feasible selections**.
>
> The QUBO's own optimum **is** the true coverage optimum.
>
> **Formulation gap: 0 cells (0.0%)**
> Correlation between QUBO score and true coverage: 0.929
>
> The mathematics is sound.

**Speaker notes**

Before testing the solver, I had to separate two questions: is the
formulation right, and does the solver work? These have different answers, and
conflating them is the usual way this kind of study goes wrong.

For a small enough instance you can check the formulation exhaustively. I took
sixteen real pads, enumerated every legal way to choose six of them — two
thousand three hundred and twenty-four — and compared what the QUBO thinks is
best to what is actually best.

They are the same. The formulation gap is zero. Whatever happens next is about
the solver, not the mathematics.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/experiments/12_qubo_solver.py

## Slide 8 — Finding 1 continued: the QUBO route loses

**On the slide**

> | Problem size | Proven optimum | QUBO annealing | Direct classical search |
> |---|---:|---:|---:|
> | 16 pads, pick 6 | 87 | 79 (90.8%) | **85 (97.7%)** |
> | 24 pads, pick 8 | 162 | 149 (92.0%) | **160 (98.8%)** |
> | 40 pads, pick 10 | 198 | 170 (85.9%) | **194 (98.0%)** |
>
> Classical: **more accurate, 35–72× faster**, and the gap widens with size.
>
> Every annealing sample was feasible — this is not a tuning failure.

**Speaker notes**

This is the uncomfortable result.

I ran the QUBO through an annealing sampler — the dwave-samplers package, which
is the same software interface a real D-Wave machine is driven through. The
QUBO object would submit to hardware unchanged.

Against the proven optimum, annealing reaches eighty-six to ninety-two
percent. A plain greedy search followed by local swaps reaches ninety-eight
percent — and does it in under half a second, where annealing takes three to
thirty-five seconds.

Every annealing sample satisfied the constraints, so this is not a bad penalty
weight. The formulation works. The route of converting to a QUBO and annealing
it is, on this problem at these sizes, worse than not converting at all.

For the hardware question: a quantum result here has to beat a 0.35-second
classical method already within two percent of optimal. And the embedding cost
is severe — forty pads already means 820 variables and 33,689 couplings on a
dense graph.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/experiments/12_qubo_solver.py — figures `fig25_qubo_gap_decomposition.png`, `fig26_qubo_solver_convergence.png`

## Slide 9 — Finding 2: the golden ratio is not where the performance comes from

**On the slide**

> Named layouts (golden spiral, phyllotaxis…) change **two things at once**:
> how radius grows, and how angle advances. A ranking cannot say which matters.
>
> So: 5 radial laws × 5 angular laws, everything else fixed, replicated.
>
> | Source of variation in UV coverage | Share |
> |---|---:|
> | **Radial law** | **84.6%** |
> | Angular law | 11.2% |
> | Interaction | 3.5% |
>
> Uniform-in-area vs Gaussian radial: **factor of 2.7**
> Golden angle vs a *degenerate* rational angle (5 bearings only): **2.5%**

**Speaker notes**

The project started from a paper on golden-spiral layouts. The implicit claim
is that the golden angle is what makes them good.

But a golden spiral changes two things at once — how far out each antenna sits,
and what bearing it is placed at. If it wins, you cannot say which of those
two was responsible.

So I separated them. Five ways of distributing radius, five ways of
distributing angle, all twenty-five combinations, with everything else held
fixed and jittered replicates so there is a proper error term. Then a standard
variance decomposition.

For UV coverage it is not close. The radial distribution explains
eighty-five percent of the variation. The angular law explains eleven. The
golden angle beats a deliberately bad angle — one that only ever uses five
distinct bearings — by two and a half percent.

The radial distribution is what matters. The angular law is a second-order
correction. This changes what advice we would give someone building an array.

I should add: sidelobes behave differently — there the two factors are
comparable and the interaction is large. So this is specific to coverage.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/experiments/16_factorial_layout.py — figures `fig33_factorial_heatmap.png`, `fig34_factorial_variance.png`

## Slide 11 — A real array design for Ladakh

**On the slide**

> Terrain-constrained, three configurations, shared pad field at Hanle.
> Array centre placed on the plain, **2,049 m** from the observatory marker.
>
> | | Compact | Intermediate | Extended |
> |---|---:|---:|---:|
> | Baselines | 32–232 m | 64–878 m | 338–2,788 m |
> | Resolves to | 1.16″ | 0.31″ | **0.096″** |
> | Sees up to | **5.01″** | 2.50″ | 0.48″ |
> | Ground relief | 11 m | 9 m | 15 m |
>
> Continuous coverage **0.096″ to 5.01″** — a 52× range.
> Output: pad coordinates with latitude, longitude, elevation, in CASA format.

**Speaker notes**

This is the part that produces something a surveyor could use.

Real elevation data, filtered for buildable slope, confined to one landform so
the array does not straddle a cliff. Then the optimiser chooses pad positions.
The outputs are latitude, longitude and elevation for every pad, and a file in

Why three configurations: sixteen antennas spread over three kilometres cannot

this the same way, by moving antennas between configurations. I checked that
the three overlap, so nothing falls between them.

Hanle's advantage: all three configurations sit within fifteen metres of ground
relief. The drier site A needs fifty-seven. Hanle's plain is genuinely flat.

What this is not: a construction plan. No land rights, roads, power, or
geotechnics. It says so on every output file.

## Slide 12 — Replacing an invented score with a derived one

**On the slide**

> **Problem:** the array was scored by "occupied UV cells" — a number chosen for
> convenience. Optimising a score you invented is circular.
>
> **Fix:** derive the weighting from a real measurement.
>
> Source model (HL Tau disc with a gap) → visibility → Fisher information →
> Cramér-Rao bound on the error bar.
>
> | Parameter measured | Error-bar improvement | Cost in UV cells |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | Gap depth | 10.4% | 43% |
> | **Gap radius** | **16.0%** | 9% |
> | Gap width | 10.9% | 45% |
>
> Arrays now compare by the error bar they deliver on a physical quantity.

**Speaker notes**

Throughout, the score was the number of UV cells an array touches. Nobody
builds a telescope to touch UV cells. I picked that metric, so optimising it
proves nothing.

The replacement comes from estimation theory. Take a real source — HL Tau, a
young star with rings, from the ALMA long-baseline campaign. Ask: how well can
this array measure the depth, or radius, or width of a ring? Fisher information
tells you exactly how much each UV sample is worth for that question. The
weighting falls out with nothing left to tune.

The result: optimising for the measurement beats optimising for cell count by
ten to sixteen percent on the error bar. But it costs coverage — up to
forty-five percent of UV cells for depth — because a measurement-optimised
array piles baselines into the informative band. That is the
estimation-versus-imaging trade-off, quantified.

One structural bonus: this objective is linear in the baseline variables, so
unlike coverage it needs no Rosenberg quadratization at all.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/src/thz_opt/science/disk_gap.py and `experiments/13_science_case.py`

## Slide 13 — Does UV coverage actually predict image quality?

**On the slide**

> Everything above optimises a **predictor** of image quality. Nobody had made
> an image.
>
> Pipeline: sky → visibilities → sample on real coverage → noise → CLEAN →
> compare to truth. Transform pair verified to **2 × 10⁻¹⁶**.
>
> | Sky model | Cells predict structure | Cells predict flux |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | Point source | **+1.000** | +1.000 |
> | Close double | **+1.000** | +0.600 |
> | Smooth Gaussian | **+1.000** | **0.000** |
> | Disc with gap | **+1.000** | +0.300 |

**Speaker notes**

Every result so far ranks arrays by a stand-in for image quality — cells,
sidelobes, an error bound. None of them is a picture. The whole framework
assumes that improving those numbers improves the image. I built an end-to-end
imaging pipeline to test that.

First I verified the transform itself: sampling every cell returns the input
sky to one part in ten to the sixteen. If that were off, every fidelity number
would be silently biased by the same factor.

Then the answer. For structural accuracy — does the picture have the right
shape — occupied cells predict perfectly. Rank correlation of exactly one
against every sky model. The cheap metric is validated for what we built it
for.

For flux — did we recover the right total brightness — it predicts nothing on
extended sources. Zero correlation on a smooth Gaussian. Next slide shows why.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/experiments/14_imaging_validation.py

## Slide 14 — The failure a cell count cannot see

**On the slide**

> | Array | Occupied UV cells | Flux recovered on a smooth Gaussian |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | Searched, maximum cells | 5,204 | **42%** |
> | Golden spiral, snapped to pads | 3,350 | **91%** |
>
> 55% more cells. **Less than half the source.**
>
> Maximising cells pushes antennas to long baselines → extended emission is
> resolved out. No cell count, and no objective built on one, can detect this.
>
> **This qualifies our own headline:** the +10.1% from search is a gain in
> *structure*, not in *flux*.

**Speaker notes**

Here is the concrete case. The array that maximises cells has fifty-five
percent more of them than the golden spiral — and recovers forty-two percent of
a smooth source where the spiral recovers ninety-one.

The reason is physical. Maximising cells drives antennas outward, because there
are more cells at large UV radius. Long baselines resolve out extended
emission. The array literally cannot see the smooth part of the source, and a
cell count has no way of noticing.

I want to flag this against our own result. The ten percent improvement from
search on real ALMA pads is real, and it is an improvement in structural
accuracy. It is not an improvement in flux recovery. The paper now says so,
because a reviewer would find it otherwise.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/figures/fig30_imaging_metric_agreement.png

## Slide 15 — Instrumental limits the coverage metrics cannot express

**On the slide**

> Primary beam, bandwidth smearing, time smearing — added and measured.
>
> **Which one limits the usable field depends on the observation:**
>
> | Setup | Bandwidth limit | Time limit | Primary beam | Binds |
> |---|---:|---:|---:|---|
> | Spectral line | 108″ | 427″ | 9.7″ | primary beam |
> | Narrow continuum | 5.4″ | 142″ | 9.7″ | **bandwidth** |
> | Wide continuum | **0.67″** | 28″ | 9.7″ | **bandwidth** |
>
> **The tension:** both smearing radii scale with the synthesised beam.
> Extend the array for resolution → the usable field shrinks in proportion.

**Speaker notes**

Three effects were missing from the model, and all three get worse exactly
where this project pushes — long baselines, short wavelengths.

The result is that which limit binds the field of view is not fixed. For
spectral-line work it is the dish. For wide continuum it is bandwidth smearing,
and the usable field collapses to two-thirds of an arcsecond.

The design consequence is a trade none of our coverage objectives can see:
both smearing limits are proportional to the synthesised beam, and the beam
shrinks as the array grows. Every extra metre of baseline that improves
resolution shrinks the usable field by the same proportion.

Also, with the real atmospheric opacity, sensitivity is now in physical units —
about thirty microjanskys in an hour at Hanle in good winter conditions.

**Source:** https://github.com/manas-vamsi/thz-quantum-optimization/blob/main/experiments/15_instrumental_limits.py

## Slide 16 — A tool anyone can use

**On the slide**

> python design_array.py --site alma  --n 20 --dish 12
> python design_array.py --site hanle --n 16 --dish 8 --max-baseline 3000

> - `run.yaml` — every parameter, for an identical rerun

> violates the antenna count, so there is nothing to penalise. QUBO penalties

**Speaker notes**

Everything is wrapped in a command-line tool. Give it a site, how many
antennas, the dish size, and it returns pad coordinates and every pairwise
distance.

One design choice worth explaining. There is no option to type in a penalty
weight. The classical search swaps one antenna in and one out, so the count
never changes and there is no constraint to penalise. On the QUBO route the
penalties exist, but the code derives the smallest safe value — and a
hand-entered value below it makes the formulation wrong silently, with no
error. So the tool shows the derived value rather than inviting a wrong one.

| **WVR corrected, calm** | **70 μm** | **0.738** | **0%** |
| WVR corrected, windy | 140 μm | 0.316 | 90% |

Water-vapour radiometer correction is the difference between an array where
almost nothing is coherent and one where everything is. Wind alone moves the
result across most of that range.

A correction worth recording: an earlier version assumed 1 mm path RMS and
concluded long baselines were unusable. The measured value is 70 μm — fourteen
times smaller. That conclusion was an artefact of the assumption.

### B3 — Algorithms used

| Algorithm | What it does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Greedy removal | Start with all pads, drop the least useful one at a time | `optimize/heuristics.py` |
| Greedy addition | Start with one, add the best each step | same |
| 1-swap local search | Exchange one pad in for one out while it improves | same |
| Simulated annealing | Metropolis over swaps, auto-calibrated temperature | same |
| MILP (HiGHS) | Exact, with a proven dual bound | `optimize/exact.py` |
| QUBO annealing | `dwave-samplers`, same interface as D-Wave hardware | `experiments/12_qubo_solver.py` |
| Rosenberg quadratization | Quartic → quadratic | `qubo/baseline_qubo.py` |
| Högbom CLEAN | Image deconvolution | `imaging/clean.py` |
| Two-way ANOVA | Variance decomposition | `experiments/16_factorial_layout.py` |

### B4 — What is deliberately not claimed

- No quantum **hardware** was used. The QUBO ran on a classical annealer behind the hardware interface.
- No real **visibility data** was processed. Imaging is simulated, correctly, but simulated.
- Sections 4.1–4.6 of the manuscript use **synthetic pad fields**, by design, so N and extent stay fixed across comparisons.
- Ladakh **phase stability does not exist as a measurement**. It needs an interferometer on site for years. All Ladakh coherence figures are transferred from ALMA and labelled a model.
- The Ladakh design models **terrain only** — no land rights, roads, power, geotechnics.
